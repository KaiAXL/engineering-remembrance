Research Brief

Engineering Remembrance

Reconstructing one family, and increasingly a vanished Subcarpathian community, from the fragmented records the Holocaust left behind, using recently released online archives and AI-assisted record linkage, a repeatable method that finds what standard searches miss.

Lajos Helman322

Zacharias Helman324

One surname. One shtetl. One trade. Together again, over 80 years later. Buchenwald intake, 2 June 1944: the brothers Lajos and Michal Helman and their uncle Zacharias of Tiszabogdány, three Hungarian Jews selected together at Auschwitz and sent on as one. The numbers 322, 323, 324 are the sequence stamped on their intake photographs, the camp’s own record that three men of one family stood one behind the other in the same line and were photographed shoulder to shoulder, about a week after being processed at Auschwitz. Three surviving photographs, from three separate cards but one family, are a rare find; rarer still is the full deportation-to-liberation path reconstructed from them. The first images our family had ever seen of any of them.

Method

01

Normalize the name. Treat orthographic and cross-linguistic spelling drift (Helman, Hellmann, Lando, Landau) as expected noise, not contradiction, the point where ordinary name-search fails.

02

Reconstruct the logistics. Locate each person in time and place through the administrative trail of arrest, ghetto, transport, and camp registration.

03

Correlate with AI. Link one individual across archives, languages, and scripts at a scale infeasible by hand.

04

Verify and weigh. Check every linkage against the primary document, and weigh evidence by source type and independence. AI accelerates the search; human judgment decides.

1 card → 300+ documents Starting from the one prisoner card our family had, the method recovered these three among more than 300 documents in all, tracing the family from a Subcarpathian village through Auschwitz and Buchenwald to the survivors’ reunion in Budapest, 1945.

What is new

The contribution is methodological. Work of this kind has traditionally required a trained specialist proceeding record by record; this project formalizes it into a reproducible protocol for AI-assisted linkage across fragmented, multilingual sources, applied to region-wide collections (Arolsen, census records, JewishGen vital registers) usable by any family from the region, not this one alone. Its evidentiary standard is convergence: an identification is admitted only where independent record types (camp forms, civil registers, census, victim databases, testimony) corroborate one another without contradiction, each linkage verified against the primary document and weighted by source type and independence.

Next: extending the reconstruction across the Subcarpathian region, and preparing further identifications, including within the Auschwitz Album, for submission to Yad Vashem. The method is also being documented as a public resource, so other descendants can apply it.
