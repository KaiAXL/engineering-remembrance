Databases

Where to actually look

The main collections used in this case, what each holds, what it costs, and the one thing that makes each one work. Most of them are free.

If you only do one thing

Go to Arolsen, type your surname, and then type it again the way a German clerk would have spelled it. No account, no fee. It is the largest collection of Nazi persecution records in the world and it is open to anyone.

Start here

Arolsen Archives Free, no account

Thirty million documents on Nazi persecution: camp registration cards, transport and transfer lists, labor deployment, death books, displaced-persons and postwar tracing files.

Search the town as well as the person. A village name returns the neighbors, and the neighbors’ records will place your family.

Covers: People persecuted from every country under Nazi rule: Romania, Hungary, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Ukraine, Slovakia, Germany, Austria and the rest of Europe.

Yad Vashem, Central Database of Shoah Victims’ Names Free

Pages of Testimony, filled in by surviving relatives, so they carry parents, spouses, ages and hometowns that no camp form records.

Read the submitter. A Page filed by a relative is a living-family lead, and the address and date on it are where to start looking for descendants.

Covers: Victims from every country: Romania, Hungary, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Ukraine, Slovakia, Germany, Austria and the rest of Europe.

USHMM Collections Free

Holds RG-31.136, a large digitized selection of the Subcarpathian regional archive’s Jewish community records, roughly 488,000 images, browsable by collection and file.

Use JewishGen to find the fund and file number first, then come here to read the actual page.

Covers: Worldwide, with the Subcarpathian civil registers (today Ukraine).

Births, marriages and deaths

JewishGen Free with registration

Indexed vital records for Subcarpathia, Máramaros, Hungary, Galicia and much of eastern Europe, plus the 1921 Czechoslovak census and the burial registry.

Search surname plus town, never a given name first. Given names collide across languages; a village does not.

Covers: Most of Europe: Romania, Hungary, Ukraine, Slovakia, Poland, Czechoslovakia and more.

Sub-Carpathia Genealogy Index free, a small fee per scan

The transcription project behind the JewishGen Subcarpathia records, run by Roberta Solit. Also holds more than 25,000 tombstone photographs that are not on JewishGen.

Buy the scan when you need the image, but check first whether the index alone answers your question. It often does.

Covers: Subcarpathia: today western Ukraine, once Czechoslovakia and Hungary.

Gesher Galicia Search free, membership for scans

The All-Galicia Database, for anywhere that was Austrian Galicia: today’s south-eastern Poland and western Ukraine.

Search by locality when a surname returns nothing. Many towns are indexed by place but not by name.

Covers: Galicia: today south-eastern Poland and western Ukraine.

JRI-Poland Free

Jewish records indexing for Poland, and the natural companion to Gesher Galicia for anything on the Polish side of the border.

Covers: Poland.

FamilySearch Free with an account

Billions of indexed records and millions of filmed register pages from around the world, including civil and church registers that exist nowhere else online.

Many register films are browse-only, not indexed: open the film for the town and year and page through it. That is how I saw Szerene’s 1910 birth entry.

Covers: worldwide, with deep coverage of eastern Europe’s registers.

Census and maps

Hungaricana Free

Hungarian and Czechoslovak archival collections, including the 1921 census sheets that put a whole household on one page: everyone under one roof, with ages and birthplaces.

A census sheet is the fastest way to prove two people were family, because it shows them living together.

Covers: Hungary and the lands once part of it: Slovakia, Transylvania (now Romania), Subcarpathia (now Ukraine), Vojvodina.

Camps

Terezín Memorial database Free

Prisoner and transport cards for Terezín, including people who arrived on the last evacuation transports in spring 1945 and were liberated there.

Covers: Prisoners deported to Terezín from across Europe, including Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Germany and Austria.

Gross-Rosen Museum database Free

Prisoner records for Gross-Rosen and its very large network of subcamps, which absorbed many Hungarian Jews in 1944.

Covers: Prisoners from across Europe, many of them Hungarian Jews in 1944.

Ancestry, Arolsen Concentration Camp Lists 1939–1944 Subscription, often free at a public library

The same Arolsen camp registers, indexed and name-searchable by Ancestry, with the roster page images attached. A search here sometimes surfaces a person the free Arolsen search misses, and it holds the alphabetical intake rolls that let you read the names on either side of your own. The three men in this case sit one under the other in its 2 June 1944 roll, entries 322, 323 and 324.

Search the surname the German way too, and always open the image rather than stopping at the index card. Ancestry also carries the US census, naturalization and passenger records that document a survivor’s life after 1945, and many public libraries provide free access. The online list is of prisoners who died there; for someone who survived, send the museum its prisoner inquiry form (karta poszukiwań więźnia).

Covers: Camp registers for prisoners from every country: Romania, Hungary, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Ukraine, Slovakia, Germany, Austria and the rest of Europe.

Cemeteries and gravestones

JewishGen KehilaLinks Free

Volunteer-built town pages. For this region they carry Marshall Katz’s photographs and transcriptions of about a hundred Subcarpathian cemeteries, taken over several summers at his own expense.

A gravestone names the father. When the civil registers run out, a stone can give you the next generation up.

Covers: Most of Europe: Romania, Hungary, Ukraine, Slovakia, Poland, Czechoslovakia and more.

JOWBR, the burial registry Free

Indexed burials worldwide, searchable by name, with plot positions and often the father’s name from the inscription.

Covers: Jewish burials worldwide.

Find a Grave Free

Volunteer photographs of gravestones, mostly in the United States, often with dates, family links and an obituary.

Survivors’ stones in America often carry the Hebrew name and the father’s name, the link back to Europe.

Covers: mostly the United States, with Israel and much of the world.

Testimony and voices

USC Shoah Foundation Visual History Archive Many testimonies viewable free

More than fifty thousand recorded survivor testimonies, indexed by person, place and topic, so you can find someone who was in the same village or on the same transport.

Search the town. A neighbor’s testimony often describes your family by name.

Covers: Survivors from every country, in more than 40 languages.

Centropa Free

Long interviews with central European Jewish families, published with their own photographs and captions naming everyone in the frame.

Covers: Central and eastern Europe, including Romania, Hungary, Slovakia, Ukraine and Poland.

Ghetto Fighters’ House Archives Free to search

A museum and archive in Israel with a large photograph collection of the ghettos, the camps and the displaced-persons years, including the children’s centers in Bavaria. The Dror Habonim photograph of Helen and Ibi is theirs, number 19650.

Search the place and year, not the name: most people in group photographs were never named. Ask for a better scan if you think you see your family.

Covers: photographs and documents from across Europe, with Israel’s survivor collections.

DEGOB protocols Free

Thousands of statements taken from Hungarian survivors in 1945, within months of liberation, and the Kassa log of deportation trains.

Covers: Hungary and the lands it ruled in 1944, including Northern Transylvania (now Romania), Subcarpathia (now Ukraine) and southern Slovakia.

Family trees and DNA

Geni Search free, some features paid

One shared world family tree, built by its users. Many Holocaust-era families have been entered by their descendants.

Treat a tree as a lead, not a record. Check every person on it against a document before you add them.

Covers: worldwide.

MyHeritage Search free, subscription for records

Family trees and historical records, strong for Israeli and eastern European families, with DNA matching for those who test there.

Look at who else is researching the same surname and town. Their tree may hold the cousin you are missing.

Covers: worldwide, strong for Israel and Europe.

GEDmatch Free, some tools paid

Upload a DNA test from any company and compare it with people who tested elsewhere.

DNA helped me only a little. It can confirm a link the records suggest, but it rarely finds one on its own for these families.

Covers: anyone who uploads a DNA test.

Books, directories and community records

Genealogy Indexer Free

Full-text search across digitized business directories, yizkor books, school and military lists from central and eastern Europe.

This is where you establish a negative. Finding that a family is absent from three editions of a directory is evidence about how they lived.

Covers: Central and eastern Europe, including Romania, Hungary, Poland, Ukraine and Slovakia.

Yizkor books Free

Memorial books written by a town’s own survivors after the war. They give the roundup date, the route, the names of the rabbi and the shochet, and how many households there were.

Many are also scanned in full on archive.org even when the translation on JewishGen is partial.

Covers: Towns across eastern Europe, including Romania, Hungary, Poland, Ukraine and Slovakia.

YIVO Archives Free to search

The YIVO Institute in New York holds the largest archive of eastern European Jewish life: community records, landsmanshaftn (hometown society) papers, letters and photographs.

Search the town and its society. Hometown societies in America kept member lists and memorial books.

Covers: eastern Europe and the Jewish immigrant world in America.

Before you start

Write down every spelling of the surname, and the mother’s maiden name as well. Almost every collection above is a name index, and a name index only returns what you type. The method explains why that one step matters more than which database you choose.
