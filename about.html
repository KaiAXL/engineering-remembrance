The Researcher

Their names are in the records. I connect them to their families.

Millions of Holocaust-era records are online, and the people in them are scattered across archives, in a dozen languages, under spellings their own families wouldn’t recognize. I link each record to the right person, and each person to the family still looking for them, and I teach families and institutions to do the same.

Now AI can help read them, and the children of survivors are still here to say who people were.

Samantha Seligman-Grajewski

Third generation. Granddaughter of Helen Lando Helman of Tiszabogdány. Founder of Engineering Remembrance.

I’m a process engineer, completing an M.S. in Chemical Engineering at Stanford. I had no training in Holocaust research or genealogy when I started, and I still have none. What I had was a way of thinking about systems, and a grandmother whose family had been reduced to a village name and a handful of first names.

For most of my life we had nothing of Helen’s family. No photograph, no grave, no date. The family held one Buchenwald prisoner card for her father, which only reached us in 2019, when the Arolsen Archives put their holdings online, and assumed that was the end of what could be known. Helen’s story is on the home page.

Helen and my grandfather Tibi Katz helped raise me. Helen was a super grandma: she was happiest with her grandchildren, and she was the kindest, best person I have ever met. I chose UCLA for my undergraduate degree so I would never be far from them, and when Helen died, I moved in with Tibi and lived with him until he died. He was my favorite roommate. You would never have known what either of them had been through. Helen would tell anyone; that’s why she spoke at schools. Tibi didn’t talk about the war the way she did, but eventually he opened up to me.

I started looking properly in 2026. I do this research from Hawai‘i, about as far from Europe as you can get. I speak only English. The records are in Hungarian, Czech, German, Russian, Yiddish, Hebrew and more, so I use AI to help me read and translate them, and then I check what it tells me against the page.

Record linkage is a data problem: names that drift across languages, dates that don’t agree, one person split across twenty files. Engineers take a finished thing apart to see how it was made. I do the same with a family: I reverse-engineer what happened from the records the system left behind. That’s why this is called Engineering Remembrance. If I can learn it, you can too.

What she said about me

In 2008 Helen sat for a recorded interview. Near the end, the interviewer asks about her grandchildren.

I was sixteen. I had said once, at her kitchen table, that I wanted to go to Stanford, and then I never applied. I went to UCLA instead, to stay close to them. Eighteen years later I applied to Stanford for a master’s and was admitted, and that July I listened to this tape again and heard what she had done with a passing remark from a teenager.

What I found

One Buchenwald card became 2,008 records from 26 archives and databases, in 12 languages.

Every finding is shown on its document and held only where independent kinds of record agree. What is probable is labeled probable. The method is written up so anyone can repeat it.

Bio and downloads

Samantha Seligman-Grajewski is a third-generation researcher bringing data methods to Holocaust genealogy, and the founder of Engineering Remembrance. She is a process engineer and is completing an M.S. in Chemical Engineering at Stanford. Starting from one Buchenwald card, she recovered her grandmother Helen’s family from 2,008 records in 26 archives and databases, in 12 languages. In October 2026 she co-presented a workshop with Jude Richter (USHMM) at the World Federation of Jewish Holocaust Survivors and Descendants conference in Philadelphia.
