Helen’s voice

Every word Helen speaks here is cut from her own recordings: tapes made in 2008, and a talk she gave in the early 2000s. The words on screen are transcripts of those clips.

My voice and the music

Every voice here is a real recording: my own, Samantha Seligman-Grajewski, and Helen’s. Music: Antonín Dvořák, “Goin’ Home,” performed by the United States Air Force Band (public domain).

Who is who

The people on Helen’s road, and how each was related to her. The road pauses while this is open.

The family on one page, from the registers, the 1921 census and Pages of Testimony. Liba Koppelman as Miksa’s mother is probable; Feige’s first husband is not known; Yaffa is known from family testimony.

  • Helen: Helena Landó Helman, later Helen Katz. Born 13 April 1929 in Tiszabogdány, the only survivor of her family. Died 2013.

Her parents

  • Zacharias Helman, her father, 1901–1944. A wagon driver in the village. Buchenwald, then Magdeburg, then sent back to Auschwitz and killed in October 1944.
  • Roza Landó, her mother, born about 1903. Murdered at Auschwitz with five of her children, 26 May 1944. After the war Helen registered under her surname.

Her sisters and brothers

  • Hinda (Hermina), born 1927 · Izak, 1932 · Majer, 1934 · Adela, 1936 · Yiddel, 1940. Murdered at Auschwitz, 26 May 1944.
  • Cecilie, born 1938, died in 1940, aged two.

Her father’s family

  • David Helman, her uncle, her father’s younger brother, born 1902. Crossed into the Soviet Union; according to the village’s yizkor book, he fell at Stalingrad.
  • Lajos (born 1924) and Michal (born 1925) Helman, her cousins. Their father, Miksa, was her father’s half-brother; their mother, Hudia, was her father’s half-sister. Photographed at Buchenwald on the same day as her father; both survived. Their aid forms place them in Budapest in the summer of 1945, when a cousin found Helen and told her what had happened to her family; which cousin it was is still open.
  • Their brothers and sisters: Miksa and Hudia had fourteen children. Three of them, Sara, Tzvi and Yaakov, had helped found a kibbutz in Israel. Helen lost touch with them after she came to America; we found each other again in 2026.

In Budapest

  • Cecilie Müller, born Dikker, her great-aunt, who took her in when her mother brought her to Budapest in 1943.
  • Szerene Sperber, Cecilie’s daughter, whom Helen lived with at Király utca 6. Deported to Dachau in 1944; she survived.

Her best friend

  • Ibi Weinberger, her best friend. They met in the Dror youth group in 1945 and were together at Indersdorf. Helen sailed for America in 1947; tuberculosis kept Ibi in Germany until 1951. They stayed best friends for nearly seventy years.

Her husband’s family

  • Tibi Katz, my grandfather. Helen met him around 1966 and married him a few years later.
  • Ibi Katz, Tibi’s sister, who lived a few doors up at Király utca 12 in 1944. A different Ibi from her best friend.
  • Eva, Helen’s friend and Tibi’s niece, who introduced them.
